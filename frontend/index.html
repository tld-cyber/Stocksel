  • The sparkline traces the year in weekly closing prices, and the red dot marks the week that set the low. There is no axis; it is there to show the shape of the year at a glance.
  • Xnow is the current price. It is shaded red while the stock sits inside the selected within range, and shaded darker when it has fallen below the floor entirely.
  • Xhigh is the highest price the stock traded during the same 52 weeks — what it is worth if the floor holds and the stock recovers.
  • Xlow is the lowest price it traded during the 52 weeks ending last Friday. The current week is deliberately excluded, which is what allows the price to fall through the floor and D% to go negative.
  • When is the number of days since that low was set. A low from months ago is one the stock has stayed above since. A low from a few days ago usually means the stock is still going down and simply has not stopped yet.
  • tch counts the weeks in which the price came back within 10% of the floor — the range set by within, not a fixed figure, so changing it changes this column. A count of 1 means the stock reached that price once and never went back — the low was a one-off. A high count means it has dropped to that price (within the selected range) and recovered again and again, so the price is one where people have repeatedly been willing to buy.
  • D% is how far Xnow sits above Xlow. It is coloured on the same rule as Xnow: strong red once the stock has broken through the floor, softer red while it sits inside the selected range, and plain beyond it.
  • Xlow−5, Xlow−10 and Xlow−20 are the prices 5, 10 and 20% below the floor. They show how far the stock would fall if it keeps dropping — they are not prices to aim to buy at.
  • Vol/day is the typical daily volume, taken as the median week divided by five rather than a single session, which is too easily distorted by whatever news moved the stock.

Splits are adjusted for — but not reliably. Prices are split-adjusted, so a reverse split normally lines history up with today. Some are missed: those show a 14–86× jump between consecutive weeks, which no real move produces, and the row is tagged CHECK LOW.

BROKE = the stock is now cheaper than its lowest price of the past year. It is falling, not steadying. 52W = a recent listing whose window is shorter. THIN = under 50k shares a day.

The controls above the table — every one narrows what is listed; none of them changes a stock's numbers.

  • NEAR / FAR — which end of the list. NEAR shows stocks close to, at, or below their lowest price of the year; FAR shows the ones that have climbed furthest away from it. Names trading under the floor stay in NEAR, tagged BROKE and shaded darker.
  • ticker — jumps straight to one stock. It deliberately ignores the within band, so searching a name that sits far above its floor still finds it rather than returning nothing.
  • within — the range this page is about: a stock qualifies while Xnow is no more than that far above Xlow. It drives three things at once — which rows are listed, the red shading, and tch, which counts the weeks price came back inside that same range. Widening the band therefore raises the touch count. Nothing is fixed at 5%.
  • show top — how many rows to draw, in the current sort order.
  • min vol/day — hides stocks that hardly trade. When only a few thousand shares change hands in a day, a small trade can move the price a long way, so the percentages stop meaning much.
  • hide CHECK LOW — on by default. Hides rows whose price history is on a different share basis to their current price, usually an unadjusted split, where D% cannot be trusted.

Rows whose current price exceeds their own 52-week high by more than half are removed outright rather than flagged: that is arithmetically impossible from one dataset, so the row would sort to the top of FAR on numbers that mean nothing.

Data retrieved from Yahoo Finance.

Already excluded: bad ticks, zero-volume phantom bars, warrants and units, ETFs, and instruments whose whole year spans under 25%. Screening only — no scores, no recommendation.
